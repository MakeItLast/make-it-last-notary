Apostille & Authentication

For documents used outside the U.S.

An apostille is a certificate that a government office attaches to your document so a foreign country will accept it as genuine. Without it, a U.S. birth certificate or diploma has no standing abroad. The rules change depending on who issued the document and which country it's going to — that's the part people get wrong, and it's the part we handle.

Why people need one

  • Marriage abroad, or registering a foreign marriage
  • Dual citizenship and residency applications
  • Foreign adoption and custody matters
  • Teaching, working, or studying overseas
  • Opening a company or bank account in another country
  • Estate and inheritance claims held abroad

Where your document has to go

  • Georgia documents (birth, marriage, death certificates, diplomas, notarized documents) → the Georgia Superior Court Clerks' Cooperative Authority (GSCCCA) in Atlanta — Georgia is one of the few states where apostilles don't go through the Secretary of State, which is exactly the kind of detail that gets DIY requests rejected
  • Documents from other states → that state's designated apostille office, handled by mail
  • Federal (FBI background checks, FDA and USDA certificates, federal court records) → the U.S. Department of State
  • Countries outside the Hague Convention → authentication first, then legalization at that country's embassy or consulate

What we do

  • Review your document and confirm it's in a form the state will actually accept
  • Notarize or obtain certified copies where they're required first
  • Prepare and submit the request, pay the government filing fees, and track it
  • Arrange certified translation when the destination country requires it
  • Return by tracked courier — domestic or international

FAQ

Questions we get every week.

General

What does a notary actually do?

A notary public is a state-commissioned official who confirms your identity, confirms you're signing willingly, and records the act in a journal. The seal doesn't mean the document is legally correct — it means the signature on it is genuine. That distinction matters, and it's why we can't tell you whether your document says the right thing.

What ID do I need?

A current, unexpired government-issued photo ID — driver's license, state ID, passport, permanent resident card, or military ID. The name on the ID has to match the name on the document. If it doesn't, call us before the appointment so we can figure out the fix.

Should I sign before you arrive?

No. The whole point is that the notary witnesses the signature. If a document is already signed, we usually can't notarize it — you'd need to sign a fresh copy. Leave every signature line blank.

Can you tell me which document I need, or fill it out for me?

No, and be cautious of anyone who says otherwise. A notary who isn't an attorney can't select documents, explain legal effect, or advise you on how to complete them. We can explain what each notarial act does and point you toward the right resource.

Do you bring witnesses?

Some documents — wills and certain deeds especially — require witnesses in addition to the notary, and the notary usually can't serve as one. Tell us in advance and we'll either arrange witnesses or tell you how many to have on hand.

Apostille

What's the difference between an apostille and authentication?

Both certify that a document is genuine. An apostille is the simplified version used between countries that joined the Hague Convention. If your destination country isn't a member, the document instead goes through authentication and then legalization at that country's embassy or consulate — more steps, longer timeline.

How long does it take?

It depends entirely on the issuing office and time of year, and can range from a few days to several weeks. We give you a realistic window at the start and tell you the moment anything moves.

Can you apostille a photocopy?

Usually not. Most offices require the original or a certified copy issued by the agency of record. If you only have a photocopy, we'll help you order a certified copy first.

Do I need a translation?

Often, and the requirements vary by country — some want the translation apostilled too. Tell us the destination country and we'll confirm before anything gets submitted.

Can you handle documents from other states?

Yes. Apostilles are handled by mail nationwide, and federal documents go through the U.S. Department of State. You don't need to be local for this service.

Weddings

Can a notary marry us in Georgia?

No — and anyone offering to should give you pause. Georgia law doesn't authorize notaries to solemnize marriages. Khra officiates as an ordained minister, which Georgia does recognize, and then handles your license and certificate with a notary's attention to detail.

How do we get the marriage license?

Apply together at a county Probate Court. If either of you lives in Georgia, you can apply in any county; if you both live out of state, apply in the county where the ceremony will happen. Bring the physical license to the ceremony — after that, we complete it and return it to the court for recording.

Do we need witnesses?

Georgia doesn't require any — it can truly be just the two of you and your officiant. If you'd like family to sign a keepsake certificate anyway, we'll bring one.

Do you do vow renewals?

Happily, and they're simpler — no license, no court paperwork, just the ceremony you want. Anniversaries, reaffirmations after a rough season, or renewals a first wedding never got to be.
